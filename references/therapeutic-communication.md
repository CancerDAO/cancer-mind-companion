# Therapeutic Communication Techniques in Cancer Care

## Overview

Therapeutic communication is a core nursing competency and a foundational intervention in psycho-oncology. It is a goal-directed, professional process in which the clinician uses specific skills to help patients identify their problems, process emotions, and engage more effectively with their care. In cancer settings, therapeutic communication has been shown to reduce patient distress, improve treatment adherence, and enhance quality of life.

**Source:** PMC12227948 — Experience of Nurses about Outcomes of Therapeutic Communication with Patients Suffering from Cancer: A Qualitative Study; *Palliat Support Care* (Iran, 2020)

---

## Core Techniques

### 1. Validation (Acknowledging the Patient's Experience)

**What it is:** Validating a patient's emotions means acknowledging that their feelings are real, understandable, and worthy of respect — without necessarily agreeing that the situation is as they describe. It communicates: "I hear you, and what you feel makes sense given what you are going through."

**Why it matters in cancer:** Patients often worry that their emotional reactions are "wrong" or "too much." Normalizing and validating their experience reduces isolation and opens space for honest dialogue.

**How to practice it:**
- Make eye contact (when culturally appropriate) and say: "I understand this is really difficult."
- Use nonverbal cues: nodding, open body posture, remaining still
- Example: Patient says "I hate all these medications. They make me feel numb." Response: "Yes, I can see why that would be frustrating. It sounds like you're going through a lot."

**Source:** NCBI Nursing Communication Techniques Table — Therapeutic Communication Techniques (NBK590036)

---

### 2. Reflection (Paraphrasing and Restating)

**What it is:** Reflection involves rephrasing the patient's words to show active listening and to help them hear their own thoughts more clearly. It includes **paraphrasing** (restating content) and **restating** (same meaning, different words).

**Paraphrasing:** Rephrases the client's words and key ideas to clarify the message and encourage additional communication.
- Example: Patient: "I feel useless to everyone." Nurse: "It sounds like you're feeling like a burden to the people around you."

**Restating:** Uses different word choices for the same content to encourage the patient to continue elaborating.
- Example: Patient: "This treatment is exhausting and I don't know how much longer I can handle it." Nurse: "So you're saying the treatment is draining you and you're not sure you can keep going?"

**Why it matters:** Reflection helps patients feel heard and can clarify ambiguous or distressing statements, enabling deeper exploration of their concerns.

**Source:** NCBI NBK590036 — Therapeutic Communication Techniques table

---

### 3. Normalization

**What it is:** Normalization involves helping the patient recognize that their emotional or physical reactions are typical responses to abnormal circumstances — not signs of weakness, failure, or mental illness.

**Why it matters in cancer:** Many patients feel ashamed or frightened by their distress, believing they "should be stronger." Normalization reduces shame, decreases psychological isolation, and encourages help-seeking.

**How to practice it:**
- "Given everything you're dealing with, it's completely understandable to feel overwhelmed."
- "Many people in your situation find themselves worrying more than usual. That's a normal reaction to a cancer diagnosis."
- "It's common to feel sad or scared — these are some of the most difficult things a person can face."

**Evidence note:** NCCN Distress Guidelines recommend that mild distress may be managed by the primary oncology team through normalization, education, and supportive listening. Only moderate-to-severe distress warrants referral to mental health professionals.

**Source:** NCCN Distress Guidelines 2023, 2024 (DIS-5, DIS-6); ASCO Adaptation Guidelines (PMC4090422)

## Additional Key Techniques

### Open-Ended Questions / Offering General Leads
Using open questions or broad invitations provides space for the patient to direct the conversation and decide what to discuss.
- "What's on your mind today?"
- "What would you like to talk about?"
- "Tell me more about how you've been feeling lately."

**Source:** NCBI NBK590036

### Providing Silence
Allowing quiet time gives the patient space for self-reflection. The nurse does not verbally respond but may use nonverbal communication to demonstrate active listening.
- **Clinical note:** In oncology, being comfortable with silence is especially important when patients receive difficult news or are processing grief and fear.

**Source:** PMC6970020 — Compassion in Practice: Difficult Conversations in Oncology Nursing

### Clarification
Asking patients to further define what they are communicating helps ensure accurate understanding and helps patients process their own ideas more thoroughly.
- "I'm not sure I understand what you mean by 'useless.' Can you give me an example?"
- "When you say you're 'not yourself,' what does that look like for you?"

**Source:** NCBI NBK590036

### Summarizing
Demonstrates active listening and allows the nurse to verify information. Ends with an invitation for correction.
- "So, you haven't been taking your medications because of the side effects of fatigue and weight gain. Is that correct?"

**Source:** NCBI NBK590036

### Empathic Responding
Responding to the emotional content of the patient's message, not just the factual content. The Acknowledge–Normalize–Partner (ANP) Framework provides a three-step structure:
1. **Acknowledge** the emotion
2. **Normalize** the reaction as understandable
3. **Partner** with the patient to explore next steps

**Evidence:** In a UCLA study, nurses trained in the ANP framework significantly improved patient-reported quality of clinician communication (mean 7.18 vs 5.04, p = 0.001) and increased frequency of values discussions (97% vs 58%, p < 0.0001).

**Source:** Anderson K et al. The Acknowledge–Normalize–Partner (ANP) Framework: A novel empathic communication tool for oncology nurses. *Palliat Support Care*. 2023. Cambridge Core.

### Giving Recognition (Validating Positive Behaviors)
Acknowledging the patient's positive health behaviors without giving patronizing compliments.
- "I noticed you took all your medications today."
- "You've been doing the breathing exercises we discussed."

**Source:** NCBI NBK590036

### Confronting
Presenting reality or gently challenging a patient's assumptions. Should be used carefully and only after a trusting relationship has been established.
- "You've mentioned several times that you haven't drunk much, but you also mentioned going out on weekends. Can we talk about that?"

**Source:** NCBI NBK590036

## The ANP Framework in Practice

The **Acknowledge–Normalize–Partner (ANP)** framework was developed at UCLA Health and validated in oncology nursing. It is designed to be learned quickly and integrated into primary palliative care practice.

**Step 1 — Acknowledge:**
- Name the emotion the patient is expressing
- "I can see this is really upsetting for you."

**Step 2 — Normalize:**
- Place the reaction in the context of the cancer experience
- "Given what you're facing, it's understandable to feel this way."

**Step 3 — Partner:**
- Collaborate on next steps together
- "What would be most helpful for you right now?"

**Source:** Anderson K. The Acknowledge–Normalize–Partner (ANP) Framework. *Palliat Support Care*. 2023. DOI: 10.1017/S1478951523000118
